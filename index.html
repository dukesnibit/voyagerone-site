THE JOURNEY

Nearly five decades away from home.

Voyager 1 left Earth on September 5, 1977. It was launched after its twin, Voyager 2, but took a faster route through the solar system and reached Jupiter and Saturn first.

Its close encounter with Saturn's moon Titan changed the spacecraft's path, sending it northward out of the main plane of the planets. From there, Voyager 1's planetary tour became something much longer: a journey toward interstellar space.

Decades later, the spacecraft is still communicating across an enormous distance. The one-light-day milestone is not the end of the mission — it is another marker on a journey that has already outlived its original five-year design by generations.

It is a distance.

A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours: 25,902,068,356 kilometers, or about 16.095 billion miles.

A message needs a day.

Radio signals travel at the speed of light. At a one-light-day distance, a signal from Earth takes about 24 hours just to reach the spacecraft. A reply would need roughly another day to come back.

It is not an official NASA project and does not represent the Voyager mission team.

What is a light-day?

A light-day is a unit of distance: how far light travels in exactly 24 hours. NASA gives that distance as 25,902,068,356 kilometers (16,094,799,096 miles). Because radio waves also travel at the speed of light, a radio signal sent across a one-light-day distance takes about 24 hours to arrive.

Why is November 18, 2026 important?

NASA lists November 18, 2026 as the date Voyager 1 will become the first human-made object to be one light-day from Earth. The milestone is expected at 2:16:07 a.m. PST, which is 6:16:07 p.m. Philippine time. It marks distance from Earth — Voyager does not stop there.

How did Voyager 1 get this far?

Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977 on a fast trajectory toward Jupiter and Saturn. It flew by Jupiter in March 1979 and Saturn in November 1980. Its close Titan encounter redirected the spacecraft northward out of the main orbital plane, after which it continued toward the edge of the Sun's heliosphere and eventually into interstellar space.

What was the Pale Blue Dot?

On February 14, 1990, Voyager 1 looked back toward the solar system and photographed Earth from billions of kilometers away as part of a family portrait of the planets. Earth appeared as a tiny point of light. After those images were taken, Voyager 1's cameras were turned off so power could be conserved for the spacecraft's continuing scientific mission.

Is Voyager 1 really in interstellar space?

Yes. Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in August 2012. The heliopause is the outer boundary of the heliosphere — the region dominated by the Sun's solar wind and magnetic influence. Crossing it made Voyager 1 the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space.
